## Lesson 1: What Is Sourdough and How to Start a Starter

_Learn the basics of sourdough and begin creating a starter._

Sourdough bread is simple in ingredients—just flour, water, and time—but it’s powered by a living culture of wild yeast and bacteria. Unlike commercial yeast, this natural leavening comes from the air and grains around you. To make sourdough, you need a starter: a mixture of flour and water that captures these microbes and keeps them alive. This lesson teaches you how to make your first starter, the foundation of all sourdough bread.

## What Is a Sourdough Starter?

A sourdough starter is a living mixture of flour and water that feeds wild yeast and lactic acid bacteria naturally found in the environment. These microbes ferment the sugars in flour, producing carbon dioxide (which makes bread rise) and organic acids (which give sourdough its tangy flavor). You don’t add any commercial yeast—you rely entirely on the microbes that grow in your starter over time.

Your starter will change daily. It starts as a simple mix of flour and water. Over days, it becomes bubbly, rises, and develops a sour smell. It’s not a recipe—it’s a living thing that needs care.

## How to Mix Your Starter

You’ll need:
- 100g of flour (unbleached all-purpose or whole wheat work well)
- 100g of water (filtered or room temperature)
- A clean glass jar with a wide mouth (at least 500ml)
- A rubber band or marker (to track rise)

**Day 1:**
1. Pour 100g of flour into the jar.
2. Add 100g of water. Stir with a wooden spoon until no dry flour remains.
3. Cover loosely with a lid or cloth (don’t seal it tightly—air needs to circulate).
4. Mark the jar’s side with a rubber band or pen at the current level to track rise.

This mixture will look like thin pancake batter. It’s not supposed to do anything yet.

## Feeding Schedule: First 5 Days

For the first 5 days, feed your starter every 12 hours. This keeps the microbes active and helps them multiply. You’ll discard half the starter before each feeding to prevent it from growing too large and to maintain balance.

**Example feeding schedule:**
- 8:00 AM: Feed the starter (discard half, add 100g flour + 100g water)
- 8:00 PM: Feed again (discard half, add 100g flour + 100g water)

Repeat this for 5 days. Each time you feed, you’re giving the microbes new food and helping them grow stronger.

## Signs of a Healthy Starter

You’ll know your starter is working when you see:
- **Bubbles:** Small bubbles forming on the surface and inside the mixture—this means yeast is active.
- **Rise:** The starter puffs up, rising 1–2 inches above its original level after feeding. It may fall back down.
- **Tangy smell:** A slightly sour, fruity, or wine-like smell. It’s not rotten—this is normal.

If your starter smells foul (like rotten eggs or vomit), it may be contaminated. Discard it and start over with fresh flour and water.

## Worked Example: Day 3 of Starter Development

Let’s say you started on Monday morning. By Wednesday at 8:00 AM, your starter has:
- 300g total volume (after two feedings)
- Visible bubbles throughout
- Risen to 1.5 inches above the original level after feeding
- A mild sour smell, not unpleasant

You discard half (150g), then add 100g flour and 100g water. After 12 hours, you’ll likely see it rise again and bubble more. This is progress.

## Try it

Now, do it yourself:

1. Take a clean jar and add 100g of flour and 100g of water. Stir until smooth.
2. Cover loosely with a lid or cloth.
3. Mark the jar with a rubber band at the current level.
4. Set a reminder to feed it every 12 hours for the next 5 days.
5. Each time, discard half the starter (about 50g), then add 100g flour and 100g water.
6. Keep it at room temperature—ideally between 70–75°F (21–24°C), but it will grow at a range of temperatures.

Don’t worry if it doesn’t bubble or rise on day 1 or 2. It takes time. Just keep feeding it.

## Recap

- A sourdough starter is a living culture of wild yeast and bacteria that leavens bread naturally.
- Mix 100g flour and 100g water in a jar, feed every 12 hours for the first 5 days, and discard half before each feeding.
- A healthy starter bubbles, rises after feeding, and smells tangy—not foul or rotten.

## Lesson 2: Maintaining and Feeding Your Starter

_Keep your starter active and ready for baking._

Sourdough starter is alive and needs care. If you want consistent, reliable bread, you must learn how to feed and store your starter properly. A well-fed, healthy starter rises predictably and gives your bread structure, flavor, and rise. This lesson shows you how to keep it active and ready to bake with.

## Feed Your Starter Correctly

Feed your starter using equal parts flour and water by weight. For example, if you use 50 grams of flour, add 50 grams of water. This 1:1 ratio by weight is standard and helps maintain balance between wild yeast and bacteria.

Use unbleached bread flour or whole wheat flour for best results. Avoid using tap water with high chlorine levels—filtered or boiled and cooled water works better.

Example:  
- You have 100g of starter.  
- Remove 100g (discard or use in pancakes).  
- Add 100g flour and 100g water.  
- Mix until no dry spots remain.  
- Cover loosely and let sit at room temperature.

Do not use a 1:1 ratio by volume (like 1 cup flour to 1 cup water). Volume varies too much with flour type and how you scoop it. Weight is more accurate.

## Store Your Starter Based on Your Baking Schedule

How often you bake determines how you store your starter.

- **Room temperature (daily use):** Feed every 12–24 hours. Keep it at 70–75°F (21–24°C) if possible. It will be active and ready to use within 4–6 hours after feeding.
- **Refrigerator (weekly use):** Feed once a week. Take it out 2–3 hours before baking to warm up and feed. It may take 12–24 hours to become active after being removed from the fridge.

Refrigeration slows fermentation. A cold starter won’t rise fast, but it stays safe and usable for weeks.

## Know When Your Starter Is Ready

A healthy starter should rise within 4–6 hours after feeding and double in size. It should have a bubbly, airy texture and smell pleasantly sour or fruity—not rotten or alcoholic.

Signs it’s ready:
- Bubbles form within 4–6 hours.
- It rises 1.5 to 2 times its original volume.
- It smells tangy but not unpleasant.

If it doesn’t rise, it may be underfed, too cold, or too old. If it smells like alcohol, it’s over-fermented—feed it sooner.

## Adjust Feeding Frequency

If you’re baking every few days, keep your starter at room temperature and feed it daily. If you bake less often, store it in the fridge and feed it once a week.

Example:  
- You bake every 5 days.  
- Feed your starter on Sunday.  
- Store it in the fridge.  
- On Thursday, take it out, feed it, and let it sit at room temperature for 4–6 hours. It should be ready to use by Friday.

If you forget to feed it for a week while in the fridge, it’s still likely fine. Just feed it twice in a row after removing it from the fridge.

## Troubleshooting Common Problems

**Weak fermentation (no rise):**  
- The starter may be too cold. Move it to a warmer spot.  
- It may be underfed. Try feeding with more flour.  
- It may be too old. Refresh it with fresh flour and water.

**Mold (fuzzy spots, unusual colors):**  
- Discard the starter and start over.  
- Use clean utensils and jars.  
- Avoid letting it dry out—keep the surface moist.

**Alcohol smell:**  
- This means it’s over-fermented. Feed it more often or use less starter when feeding.  
- Reduce the amount you keep and feed more frequently.

**Hooch (grayish liquid on top):**  
- This is normal if you haven’t fed it in a few days. Pour it off and feed the starter.  
- If it’s very dark or smells bad, discard and restart.

## Worked Example: Getting Your Starter Ready for Baking

You want to bake bread on Saturday.  
- **Friday evening:** Take starter from the fridge.  
- **Friday night:** Feed it 100g flour + 100g water.  
- **Saturday morning:** It should be bubbly, rising, and double in size.  
- **Saturday noon:** Use 100g of starter in your dough.  
- **After baking:** Discard 100g and feed with 100g flour + 100g water.  
- **Store it in the fridge** until next baking.

This routine keeps your starter active and ready.

## Try it

Grab your starter jar.  
1. Weigh 100g of starter.  
2. Discard all but 100g (or use it in a recipe).  
3. Add 100g flour and 100g water.  
4. Mix until no dry spots remain.  
5. Cover loosely and place it at room temperature.  
6. Check it every 3 hours for the next 6 hours.  
7. Note when bubbles first appear and when it starts to rise.  
8. Record the time it doubles in size.

Repeat this process once more with a different flour (like whole wheat) to see how it affects activity.

## Recap
- Feed your starter with equal parts flour and water by weight, not volume.
- Store it at room temperature if baking daily, in the fridge if baking weekly.
- A ready starter rises within 4–6 hours after feeding and smells sour, not rotten.

## Lesson 3: Mixing and Bulk Fermentation

_Prepare dough and manage the first fermentation phase._

Sourdough bread starts with a simple mix: flour, water, salt, and your active starter. Getting this step right sets the foundation for everything that follows. The way you mix and manage the first fermentation—called bulk fermentation—directly affects your bread’s rise, texture, and flavor. In this lesson, you’ll learn how to combine your ingredients with precision, use stretch and folds to build structure, and know when your dough is ready to shape.

## Measure by Weight, Not Volume  
Using a kitchen scale is the most accurate way to measure ingredients. Volume measurements (like cups) vary based on how tightly you pack flour or how much water you add. For example, 1 cup of flour can weigh anywhere from 110 to 130 grams depending on how you scoop it. This inconsistency leads to unpredictable results.  

Use the following weights for a basic 100% hydration sourdough loaf (a common beginner recipe):  
- 500 grams of bread flour (or a mix of bread and whole wheat)  
- 500 grams of water (room temperature, about 70°F / 21°C)  
- 100 grams of active sourdough starter (fed and bubbly, 100% hydration)  
- 10 grams of fine sea salt  

Weigh everything on a digital scale. This ensures consistency, especially when you’re learning. If your scale doesn’t show grams, switch to metric. Most sourdough recipes are written in grams because they’re precise.

## Mix the Dough  
Start by combining the flour and water in a large bowl. Stir with a wooden spoon or your hand until no dry flour remains—this is called autolyse. Let the mixture rest for 30 minutes. During this time, the flour absorbs water and begins to develop gluten naturally.  

After 30 minutes, add your starter and salt. Mix thoroughly by hand until the dough comes together. It will be sticky and shaggy at first. Don’t worry—this is normal.  

If you’re using a stand mixer, mix on low speed for 2–3 minutes after adding the starter and salt. But hand mixing is often better for beginners because it helps you feel the dough’s texture and develop a connection with the process.

## Perform Stretch and Folds  
Once mixed, your dough is ready for bulk fermentation. This is a 3–4 hour period at room temperature (around 70–75°F / 21–24°C) during which the dough rises and develops structure.  

Every 30 minutes, perform a stretch and fold. Here’s how:  
- Wet your hands (to prevent sticking).  
- Gently grab one side of the dough and stretch it upward.  
- Fold it over the top.  
- Rotate the bowl 90 degrees.  
- Repeat on the next side.  
- Do this for all four sides.  

This builds strength in the dough by aligning gluten strands. It also helps the dough trap gas from fermentation, which leads to a better rise and open crumb.  

Do 4–6 sets of stretch and folds over 2–3 hours. If you’re using a container with a lid, leave it open during the folds. If you’re using a bowl, cover it with a damp cloth to prevent drying.

## Know When the Dough Is Ready  
After 3–4 hours, your dough should look and feel ready. It will:  
- Be puffy and slightly jiggly when gently pressed.  
- Have visible bubbles throughout, especially near the surface.  
- Hold its shape when lifted but still feel soft and elastic.  

If it’s still very flat and sticky, it may need more time. If it’s overproofed (too airy, collapses when touched), it may have fermented too long. Temperature affects timing: warmer rooms speed up fermentation, cooler rooms slow it down. A 75°F room might take 3.5 hours; a 68°F room could take 4.5 hours.

## Worked Example  
Let’s say you’re baking at 72°F (22°C) with a 100% hydration starter.  
- You weigh: 500g flour, 500g water, 100g starter, 10g salt.  
- Mix all ingredients and autolyse for 30 minutes.  
- Perform stretch and folds every 30 minutes for 4 rounds (after 30, 60, 90, and 120 minutes).  
- After 3 hours, the dough is slightly jiggly, full of bubbles, and holds its shape when pressed.  
- You’re ready to shape it.  

This timing and behavior are typical for a room-temperature bake. Adjust based on your environment.

## Try it  
Grab your ingredients and scale. Mix 500g flour, 500g water, 100g active starter, and 10g salt in a bowl. Let it autolyse for 30 minutes. Then, perform 4 stretch and folds at 30-minute intervals. After 3 hours, check the dough. Is it puffy? Does it jiggle? Are there bubbles? Write down what you observe. If it’s not ready, wait 30 more minutes and check again.

## Recap  
- Use a kitchen scale to measure ingredients by weight for consistent results.  
- Perform stretch and folds every 30 minutes during bulk fermentation to build dough strength.  
- The dough is ready when it’s puffy, slightly jiggly, and full of bubbles—typically after 3–4 hours at room temperature.

## Lesson 4: Shaping and Proofing the Loaf

_Shape the dough and prepare it for baking._

Shaping and proofing your sourdough loaf is where the dough transforms from a floppy mass into a structured, well-risen loaf ready for the oven. A good shape holds the gas from fermentation, giving your bread a strong crumb and even rise. Proofing—whether at room temperature or in the fridge—gives the dough time to develop flavor and texture. This step is critical: a poorly shaped or under-proofed loaf can collapse or bake unevenly.

## Shape the Dough with the Palm Press Method

The palm press is a simple, effective way to create tension on the surface of your dough. This tension helps the loaf hold its shape during baking and gives it a better rise.

1. **Turn the dough out** onto a lightly floured surface. Use just enough flour to prevent sticking—too much will dry out the dough.
2. **Press down with your palm** on one side of the dough, pushing it gently into a flat oval shape.
3. **Fold the far edge toward the center**, using your fingertips to tuck it under.
4. **Repeat on the opposite side**, folding it toward the center.
5. **Rotate the dough** 90 degrees and repeat the tucking motion on the two remaining sides.
6. **Gather the dough into a ball** by pulling the edges toward the center, then tuck the seams underneath.

The goal is a smooth, tight surface. The dough should feel firm but pliable. If it springs back too quickly, let it rest for 10 minutes and try again.

## Use a Banneton or Floured Bowl for Proofing

A banneton (proofing basket) helps maintain the loaf’s shape during the final rise. If you don’t have one, a bowl lined with a floured kitchen towel works well.

- **With a banneton**: Dust it generously with rice flour (or wheat flour). Place the shaped dough, seam-side up, into the basket. The flour prevents sticking and gives a nice crust.
- **With a bowl**: Line the bowl with a clean, dry kitchen towel. Dust the towel with flour. Place the shaped dough, seam-side up, in the bowl. Cover loosely with plastic wrap or a damp cloth.

The key is to avoid letting the dough dry out. A dry surface will crack or tear during baking.

## Cover the Dough to Prevent Drying

Always cover the dough during proofing. A dry surface leads to a tough crust and poor oven spring.

- Use a clean kitchen towel, a piece of plastic wrap, or a large bowl turned upside down.
- If proofing in the fridge, wrap the banneton or bowl tightly in plastic wrap or place it in a sealed plastic bag.

Covering also helps maintain a steady humidity level, which supports fermentation and prevents skin formation.

## Proof at Room Temperature or in the Fridge

You can proof your dough in two ways:

- **Room temperature (2–4 hours)**: Ideal if you're baking the same day. The dough will rise faster, and you’ll get a more open crumb. Watch for a soft, puffy feel and a slight jiggle when you shake the banneton.
- **Cold proof (8–12 hours, or even longer)**: This is called retardation. It slows fermentation, deepens flavor, and makes the dough easier to handle. Refrigerate the dough after shaping, seam-side up, in the banneton or bowl. Let it come to room temperature for 1–2 hours before baking.

The time varies based on temperature and starter strength. A warmer kitchen will speed up proofing; a colder fridge will slow it down.

## Check for Readiness: The Poke Test

Before baking, check if the dough is ready.

- Gently poke the side of the dough with your finger.
- If the dent springs back slowly—within 2–3 seconds—the dough is ready.
- If it springs back immediately, it needs more time.
- If the dent stays, it’s over-proofed and may collapse in the oven.

## Worked Example: A 12-Hour Cold Proof

You shaped your dough at 6:00 PM. You place it in a floured banneton, seam-side up. Dust the top lightly with rice flour. Cover with a towel. Place the banneton in the fridge at 6:30 PM. The next morning, at 6:30 AM, you take it out. Let it sit at room temperature for 1.5 hours. You test it with the poke test: the dent springs back slowly. It’s ready. You score it and bake.

## Try it

Grab your dough after bulk fermentation. Turn it out onto a floured surface. Use the palm press to shape it into a tight ball. Place it seam-side up in a floured banneton or lined bowl. Cover it with a towel. If you’re using the fridge, wrap it in plastic and refrigerate for 8–12 hours. The next day, check it with the poke test. If it passes, you’re ready to bake.

## Recap

- Shape the dough using the palm press to create surface tension and a tight structure.
- Use a banneton or floured bowl to support the loaf during proofing; cover it to prevent drying.
- Proof at room temperature for 2–4 hours or in the fridge for 8–12 hours; adjust time based on your kitchen’s temperature.
- Test readiness with the poke test: slow spring-back means it’s ready to bake.

## Lesson 5: Baking and Troubleshooting

_Bake the loaf and address common problems._

Baking your first sourdough loaf is a milestone. It’s where all your preparation—feeding the starter, mixing, fermenting, shaping—comes together. But even with care, things can go wrong. Knowing how to bake properly and fix common issues will turn a disappointing loaf into a success. This lesson covers the final steps: baking with steam, scoring, and diagnosing problems.

## Preheat the Dutch Oven and Use Steam

A Dutch oven traps heat and creates steam, which helps the loaf rise and form a crisp crust. Start by preheating your Dutch oven, lid on, in a cold oven. Set the oven to 450°F (230°C). Let it heat for at least 30 minutes. This ensures the pot is hot when you add the dough, which helps the loaf expand quickly in the first minutes of baking.

To add steam, place a small metal tray or cast iron skillet on the bottom rack of the oven while it preheats. When you put the dough in, quickly pour 1 cup of hot water into the tray. The steam will help the crust stay soft long enough for the loaf to expand fully.

*Example:* If you skip preheating, the dough will stick to the pot and won’t rise well. If you don’t use steam, the crust forms too early and the loaf won’t open up properly.

## Score the Dough Before Baking

Scoring means cutting shallow lines into the dough’s surface just before baking. This controls where the loaf expands and helps create an attractive pattern.

Use a sharp blade (like a razor or lame) and cut at a 30–45 degree angle. Make 1–2 cuts, each about 1/4 inch deep. The cuts should be clean and fast—don’t drag the blade.

*Example:* If you skip scoring, the loaf may burst unpredictably, creating an uneven shape. If you score too shallow, the cuts won’t open. Too deep, and the dough may tear.

## Bake with a Covered and Uncovered Phase

Place the dough into the hot Dutch oven, seam side down. Cover with the lid. Bake for 20 minutes. This traps steam and helps the loaf rise.

After 20 minutes, remove the lid. Continue baking uncovered for 20–30 minutes. The crust will darken and crisp up.

*Example:* Baking uncovered from the start leads to a tough crust that forms too early, limiting rise. Baking covered too long can make the crust soft and gummy.

## Check Doneness with Internal Temperature

The best way to know if your loaf is done is to check the internal temperature. Use an instant-read thermometer. Insert it into the center of the loaf. It should read between 200°F and 210°F (93°C–99°C).

If it’s below 200°F, the crumb may still be wet. If it’s above 210°F, the bread may be dry or burnt.

*Example:* A loaf pulled out at 190°F may collapse or feel gummy. One baked to 220°F may be dry and hard.

## Diagnose and Fix Common Problems

Here’s how to troubleshoot your loaf:

- **Dense crumb:** Likely under-fermented. The dough didn’t rise enough during bulk fermentation or proofing. Try extending fermentation time by 30–60 minutes, or use a warmer spot (e.g., near a heater).
- **Poor rise:** Could be a weak starter, cold oven, or under-proofed dough. Make sure your starter is active and bubbly before using. Preheat the oven fully. Let the dough proof longer—up to 2 hours in a warm room.
- **Burnt crust:** Oven temperature too high, or baking uncovered too long. Try lowering the oven to 425°F (220°C) and reduce uncovered time by 5–10 minutes. Rotate the loaf halfway through if it’s browning unevenly.
- **Crust too soft:** Not enough steam or not enough uncovered baking. Use a tray with water, and bake uncovered for the full 20–30 minutes.

## Worked Example

You baked a loaf using a starter that was fed 4 hours before mixing. The dough bulk fermented for 3 hours at room temperature (70°F), then was shaped and proofed for 2 hours in the fridge. You preheated the Dutch oven for 30 minutes at 450°F, added the dough, covered it, baked 20 minutes, then uncovered for 25 minutes. The thermometer read 205°F. The crust was crisp, the crumb open and airy.

*What went right:* Proper preheating, steam, correct baking time, and internal temperature check.

*What might go wrong next time:* If the crumb is dense, check your bulk fermentation time. If the crust is burnt, reduce uncovered time or lower the oven temperature.

## Try it

Bake one loaf using the steps above. Use a thermometer to check doneness. After baking, write down:
- How long the dough proofed (in the fridge or at room temp).
- Oven temperature and preheat time.

## Recap

- Preheat your Dutch oven and use steam for a better rise and crust.
