What Is Sourdough and How to Start a Starter

Learn the basics of sourdough and begin creating a starter.

Open the slides

Sourdough bread is simple in ingredients—just flour, water, and time—but it’s powered by a living culture of wild yeast and bacteria. Unlike commercial yeast, this natural leavening comes from the air and grains around you. To make sourdough, you need a starter: a mixture of flour and water that captures these microbes and keeps them alive. This lesson teaches you how to make your first starter, the foundation of all sourdough bread.

What Is a Sourdough Starter?

A sourdough starter is a living mixture of flour and water that feeds wild yeast and lactic acid bacteria naturally found in the environment. These microbes ferment the sugars in flour, producing carbon dioxide (which makes bread rise) and organic acids (which give sourdough its tangy flavor). You don’t add any commercial yeast—you rely entirely on the microbes that grow in your starter over time.

Your starter will change daily. It starts as a simple mix of flour and water. Over days, it becomes bubbly, rises, and develops a sour smell. It’s not a recipe—it’s a living thing that needs care.

How to Mix Your Starter

You’ll need:

  • 100g of flour (unbleached all-purpose or whole wheat work well)
  • 100g of water (filtered or room temperature)
  • A clean glass jar with a wide mouth (at least 500ml)
  • A rubber band or marker (to track rise)

Day 1:

  1. Pour 100g of flour into the jar.
  2. Add 100g of water. Stir with a wooden spoon until no dry flour remains.
  3. Cover loosely with a lid or cloth (don’t seal it tightly—air needs to circulate).
  4. Mark the jar’s side with a rubber band or pen at the current level to track rise.

This mixture will look like thin pancake batter. It’s not supposed to do anything yet.

Feeding Schedule: First 5 Days

For the first 5 days, feed your starter every 12 hours. This keeps the microbes active and helps them multiply. You’ll discard half the starter before each feeding to prevent it from growing too large and to maintain balance.

Example feeding schedule:

  • 8:00 AM: Feed the starter (discard half, add 100g flour + 100g water)
  • 8:00 PM: Feed again (discard half, add 100g flour + 100g water)

Repeat this for 5 days. Each time you feed, you’re giving the microbes new food and helping them grow stronger.

Signs of a Healthy Starter

You’ll know your starter is working when you see:

  • Bubbles: Small bubbles forming on the surface and inside the mixture—this means yeast is active.
  • Rise: The starter puffs up, rising 1–2 inches above its original level after feeding. It may fall back down.
  • Tangy smell: A slightly sour, fruity, or wine-like smell. It’s not rotten—this is normal.

If your starter smells foul (like rotten eggs or vomit), it may be contaminated. Discard it and start over with fresh flour and water.

Worked Example: Day 3 of Starter Development

Let’s say you started on Monday morning. By Wednesday at 8:00 AM, your starter has:

  • 300g total volume (after two feedings)
  • Visible bubbles throughout
  • Risen to 1.5 inches above the original level after feeding
  • A mild sour smell, not unpleasant

You discard half (150g), then add 100g flour and 100g water. After 12 hours, you’ll likely see it rise again and bubble more. This is progress.

Try it

Now, do it yourself:

  1. Take a clean jar and add 100g of flour and 100g of water. Stir until smooth.
  2. Cover loosely with a lid or cloth.
  3. Mark the jar with a rubber band at the current level.
  4. Set a reminder to feed it every 12 hours for the next 5 days.
  5. Each time, discard half the starter (about 50g), then add 100g flour and 100g water.
  6. Keep it at room temperature—ideally between 70–75°F (21–24°C), but it will grow at a range of temperatures.

Don’t worry if it doesn’t bubble or rise on day 1 or 2. It takes time. Just keep feeding it.

Recap

  • A sourdough starter is a living culture of wild yeast and bacteria that leavens bread naturally.
  • Mix 100g flour and 100g water in a jar, feed every 12 hours for the first 5 days, and discard half before each feeding.
  • A healthy starter bubbles, rises after feeding, and smells tangy—not foul or rotten.

Check yourself

1. Why is it necessary to discard half of the sourdough starter before each feeding during the first 5 days?

Discarding half the starter maintains a healthy balance of microbes and prevents the buildup of too much acid, which could inhibit yeast activity.

2. What are the two main byproducts of fermentation in a sourdough starter, and what do they do?

Lactic acid provides the tangy flavor, and carbon dioxide is produced by yeast to make the bread rise.

3. How can you tell that your sourdough starter is developing properly during the first few days?

A healthy starter rises after feeding, develops visible bubbles, and emits a tangy, fruity, or wine-like aroma.

4. What is the primary reason sourdough bread does not use commercial yeast?

Sourdough bread uses naturally occurring wild yeast and bacteria from the air and flour, eliminating the need for commercial yeast.
