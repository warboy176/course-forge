Mixing and Bulk Fermentation

Prepare dough and manage the first fermentation phase.

Open the slides

Sourdough bread starts with a simple mix: flour, water, salt, and your active starter. Getting this step right sets the foundation for everything that follows. The way you mix and manage the first fermentation—called bulk fermentation—directly affects your bread’s rise, texture, and flavor. In this lesson, you’ll learn how to combine your ingredients with precision, use stretch and folds to build structure, and know when your dough is ready to shape.

Measure by Weight, Not Volume

Using a kitchen scale is the most accurate way to measure ingredients. Volume measurements (like cups) vary based on how tightly you pack flour or how much water you add. For example, 1 cup of flour can weigh anywhere from 110 to 130 grams depending on how you scoop it. This inconsistency leads to unpredictable results.

Use the following weights for a basic 100% hydration sourdough loaf (a common beginner recipe):

  • 500 grams of bread flour (or a mix of bread and whole wheat)
  • 500 grams of water (room temperature, about 70°F / 21°C)
  • 100 grams of active sourdough starter (fed and bubbly, 100% hydration)
  • 10 grams of fine sea salt

Weigh everything on a digital scale. This ensures consistency, especially when you’re learning. If your scale doesn’t show grams, switch to metric. Most sourdough recipes are written in grams because they’re precise.

Mix the Dough

Start by combining the flour and water in a large bowl. Stir with a wooden spoon or your hand until no dry flour remains—this is called autolyse. Let the mixture rest for 30 minutes. During this time, the flour absorbs water and begins to develop gluten naturally.

After 30 minutes, add your starter and salt. Mix thoroughly by hand until the dough comes together. It will be sticky and shaggy at first. Don’t worry—this is normal.

If you’re using a stand mixer, mix on low speed for 2–3 minutes after adding the starter and salt. But hand mixing is often better for beginners because it helps you feel the dough’s texture and develop a connection with the process.

Perform Stretch and Folds

Once mixed, your dough is ready for bulk fermentation. This is a 3–4 hour period at room temperature (around 70–75°F / 21–24°C) during which the dough rises and develops structure.

Every 30 minutes, perform a stretch and fold. Here’s how:

  • Wet your hands (to prevent sticking).
  • Gently grab one side of the dough and stretch it upward.
  • Fold it over the top.
  • Rotate the bowl 90 degrees.
  • Repeat on the next side.
  • Do this for all four sides.

This builds strength in the dough by aligning gluten strands. It also helps the dough trap gas from fermentation, which leads to a better rise and open crumb.

Do 4–6 sets of stretch and folds over 2–3 hours. If you’re using a container with a lid, leave it open during the folds. If you’re using a bowl, cover it with a damp cloth to prevent drying.

Know When the Dough Is Ready

After 3–4 hours, your dough should look and feel ready. It will:

  • Be puffy and slightly jiggly when gently pressed.
  • Have visible bubbles throughout, especially near the surface.
  • Hold its shape when lifted but still feel soft and elastic.

If it’s still very flat and sticky, it may need more time. If it’s overproofed (too airy, collapses when touched), it may have fermented too long. Temperature affects timing: warmer rooms speed up fermentation, cooler rooms slow it down. A 75°F room might take 3.5 hours; a 68°F room could take 4.5 hours.

Worked Example

Let’s say you’re baking at 72°F (22°C) with a 100% hydration starter.

  • You weigh: 500g flour, 500g water, 100g starter, 10g salt.
  • Mix all ingredients and autolyse for 30 minutes.
  • Perform stretch and folds every 30 minutes for 4 rounds (after 30, 60, 90, and 120 minutes).
  • After 3 hours, the dough is slightly jiggly, full of bubbles, and holds its shape when pressed.
  • You’re ready to shape it.

This timing and behavior are typical for a room-temperature bake. Adjust based on your environment.

Try it

Grab your ingredients and scale. Mix 500g flour, 500g water, 100g active starter, and 10g salt in a bowl. Let it autolyse for 30 minutes. Then, perform 4 stretch and folds at 30-minute intervals. After 3 hours, check the dough. Is it puffy? Does it jiggle? Are there bubbles? Write down what you observe. If it’s not ready, wait 30 more minutes and check again.

Recap

  • Use a kitchen scale to measure ingredients by weight for consistent results.
  • Perform stretch and folds every 30 minutes during bulk fermentation to build dough strength.
  • The dough is ready when it’s puffy, slightly jiggly, and full of bubbles—typically after 3–4 hours at room temperature.

Check yourself

1. Why is measuring ingredients by weight more reliable than using volume measurements in sourdough baking?

Volume measurements can vary significantly based on how tightly flour is packed or how much water is added, leading to inconsistent results.

2. What is the primary purpose of performing stretch and folds during bulk fermentation?

Stretch and folds align gluten strands, build dough strength, and help trap gas from fermentation, which improves rise and crumb structure.

3. During bulk fermentation, how can you tell if the dough is ready to be shaped?

A dough that is puffy, slightly jiggly, and full of bubbles indicates proper fermentation and readiness for shaping.

4. What is the purpose of the autolyse step in sourdough bread making?

Autolyse allows the flour to absorb water and begin developing gluten naturally, improving dough structure and texture.
