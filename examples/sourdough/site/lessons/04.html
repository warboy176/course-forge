Shaping and Proofing the Loaf

Shape the dough and prepare it for baking.

Open the slides

Shaping and proofing your sourdough loaf is where the dough transforms from a floppy mass into a structured, well-risen loaf ready for the oven. A good shape holds the gas from fermentation, giving your bread a strong crumb and even rise. Proofing—whether at room temperature or in the fridge—gives the dough time to develop flavor and texture. This step is critical: a poorly shaped or under-proofed loaf can collapse or bake unevenly.

Shape the Dough with the Palm Press Method

The palm press is a simple, effective way to create tension on the surface of your dough. This tension helps the loaf hold its shape during baking and gives it a better rise.

  1. Turn the dough out onto a lightly floured surface. Use just enough flour to prevent sticking—too much will dry out the dough.
  2. Press down with your palm on one side of the dough, pushing it gently into a flat oval shape.
  3. Fold the far edge toward the center, using your fingertips to tuck it under.
  4. Repeat on the opposite side, folding it toward the center.
  5. Rotate the dough 90 degrees and repeat the tucking motion on the two remaining sides.
  6. Gather the dough into a ball by pulling the edges toward the center, then tuck the seams underneath.

The goal is a smooth, tight surface. The dough should feel firm but pliable. If it springs back too quickly, let it rest for 10 minutes and try again.

Use a Banneton or Floured Bowl for Proofing

A banneton (proofing basket) helps maintain the loaf’s shape during the final rise. If you don’t have one, a bowl lined with a floured kitchen towel works well.

  • With a banneton: Dust it generously with rice flour (or wheat flour). Place the shaped dough, seam-side up, into the basket. The flour prevents sticking and gives a nice crust.
  • With a bowl: Line the bowl with a clean, dry kitchen towel. Dust the towel with flour. Place the shaped dough, seam-side up, in the bowl. Cover loosely with plastic wrap or a damp cloth.

The key is to avoid letting the dough dry out. A dry surface will crack or tear during baking.

Cover the Dough to Prevent Drying

Always cover the dough during proofing. A dry surface leads to a tough crust and poor oven spring.

  • Use a clean kitchen towel, a piece of plastic wrap, or a large bowl turned upside down.
  • If proofing in the fridge, wrap the banneton or bowl tightly in plastic wrap or place it in a sealed plastic bag.

Covering also helps maintain a steady humidity level, which supports fermentation and prevents skin formation.

Proof at Room Temperature or in the Fridge

You can proof your dough in two ways:

  • Room temperature (2–4 hours): Ideal if you're baking the same day. The dough will rise faster, and you’ll get a more open crumb. Watch for a soft, puffy feel and a slight jiggle when you shake the banneton.
  • Cold proof (8–12 hours, or even longer): This is called retardation. It slows fermentation, deepens flavor, and makes the dough easier to handle. Refrigerate the dough after shaping, seam-side up, in the banneton or bowl. Let it come to room temperature for 1–2 hours before baking.

The time varies based on temperature and starter strength. A warmer kitchen will speed up proofing; a colder fridge will slow it down.

Check for Readiness: The Poke Test

Before baking, check if the dough is ready.

  • Gently poke the side of the dough with your finger.
  • If the dent springs back slowly—within 2–3 seconds—the dough is ready.
  • If it springs back immediately, it needs more time.
  • If the dent stays, it’s over-proofed and may collapse in the oven.

Worked Example: A 12-Hour Cold Proof

You shaped your dough at 6:00 PM. You place it in a floured banneton, seam-side up. Dust the top lightly with rice flour. Cover with a towel. Place the banneton in the fridge at 6:30 PM. The next morning, at 6:30 AM, you take it out. Let it sit at room temperature for 1.5 hours. You test it with the poke test: the dent springs back slowly. It’s ready. You score it and bake.

Try it

Grab your dough after bulk fermentation. Turn it out onto a floured surface. Use the palm press to shape it into a tight ball. Place it seam-side up in a floured banneton or lined bowl. Cover it with a towel. If you’re using the fridge, wrap it in plastic and refrigerate for 8–12 hours. The next day, check it with the poke test. If it passes, you’re ready to bake.

Recap

  • Shape the dough using the palm press to create surface tension and a tight structure.
  • Use a banneton or floured bowl to support the loaf during proofing; cover it to prevent drying.
  • Proof at room temperature for 2–4 hours or in the fridge for 8–12 hours; adjust time based on your kitchen’s temperature.
  • Test readiness with the poke test: slow spring-back means it’s ready to bake.

Check yourself

1. Why is creating surface tension during shaping important for a sourdough loaf?

Surface tension traps gas from fermentation, allowing the loaf to rise evenly and develop a strong crumb structure.

2. What is the purpose of covering the dough during proofing?

Covering the dough prevents drying, which can lead to cracking and poor oven spring.

3. During a cold proof in the refrigerator, what benefit does retardation provide?

Cold proofing slows fermentation, which deepens flavor and makes the dough easier to handle.

4. What does a slow spring-back in the poke test indicate about the dough?

A slow spring-back means the dough has enough gas retention and structure to bake properly.
