Maintaining and Feeding Your Starter

Keep your starter active and ready for baking.

Open the slides

Sourdough starter is alive and needs care. If you want consistent, reliable bread, you must learn how to feed and store your starter properly. A well-fed, healthy starter rises predictably and gives your bread structure, flavor, and rise. This lesson shows you how to keep it active and ready to bake with.

Feed Your Starter Correctly

Feed your starter using equal parts flour and water by weight. For example, if you use 50 grams of flour, add 50 grams of water. This 1:1 ratio by weight is standard and helps maintain balance between wild yeast and bacteria.

Use unbleached bread flour or whole wheat flour for best results. Avoid using tap water with high chlorine levels—filtered or boiled and cooled water works better.

Example:

  • You have 100g of starter.
  • Remove 100g (discard or use in pancakes).
  • Add 100g flour and 100g water.
  • Mix until no dry spots remain.
  • Cover loosely and let sit at room temperature.

Do not use a 1:1 ratio by volume (like 1 cup flour to 1 cup water). Volume varies too much with flour type and how you scoop it. Weight is more accurate.

Store Your Starter Based on Your Baking Schedule

How often you bake determines how you store your starter.

  • Room temperature (daily use): Feed every 12–24 hours. Keep it at 70–75°F (21–24°C) if possible. It will be active and ready to use within 4–6 hours after feeding.
  • Refrigerator (weekly use): Feed once a week. Take it out 2–3 hours before baking to warm up and feed. It may take 12–24 hours to become active after being removed from the fridge.

Refrigeration slows fermentation. A cold starter won’t rise fast, but it stays safe and usable for weeks.

Know When Your Starter Is Ready

A healthy starter should rise within 4–6 hours after feeding and double in size. It should have a bubbly, airy texture and smell pleasantly sour or fruity—not rotten or alcoholic.

Signs it’s ready:

  • Bubbles form within 4–6 hours.
  • It rises 1.5 to 2 times its original volume.
  • It smells tangy but not unpleasant.

If it doesn’t rise, it may be underfed, too cold, or too old. If it smells like alcohol, it’s over-fermented—feed it sooner.

Adjust Feeding Frequency

If you’re baking every few days, keep your starter at room temperature and feed it daily. If you bake less often, store it in the fridge and feed it once a week.

Example:

  • You bake every 5 days.
  • Feed your starter on Sunday.
  • Store it in the fridge.
  • On Thursday, take it out, feed it, and let it sit at room temperature for 4–6 hours. It should be ready to use by Friday.

If you forget to feed it for a week while in the fridge, it’s still likely fine. Just feed it twice in a row after removing it from the fridge.

Troubleshooting Common Problems

Weak fermentation (no rise):

  • The starter may be too cold. Move it to a warmer spot.
  • It may be underfed. Try feeding with more flour.
  • It may be too old. Refresh it with fresh flour and water.

Mold (fuzzy spots, unusual colors):

  • Discard the starter and start over.
  • Use clean utensils and jars.
  • Avoid letting it dry out—keep the surface moist.

Alcohol smell:

  • This means it’s over-fermented. Feed it more often or use less starter when feeding.
  • Reduce the amount you keep and feed more frequently.

Hooch (grayish liquid on top):

  • This is normal if you haven’t fed it in a few days. Pour it off and feed the starter.
  • If it’s very dark or smells bad, discard and restart.

Worked Example: Getting Your Starter Ready for Baking

You want to bake bread on Saturday.

  • Friday evening: Take starter from the fridge.
  • Friday night: Feed it 100g flour + 100g water.
  • Saturday morning: It should be bubbly, rising, and double in size.
  • Saturday noon: Use 100g of starter in your dough.
  • After baking: Discard 100g and feed with 100g flour + 100g water.
  • Store it in the fridge until next baking.

This routine keeps your starter active and ready.

Try it

Grab your starter jar.

  1. Weigh 100g of starter.
  2. Discard all but 100g (or use it in a recipe).
  3. Add 100g flour and 100g water.
  4. Mix until no dry spots remain.
  5. Cover loosely and place it at room temperature.
  6. Check it every 3 hours for the next 6 hours.
  7. Note when bubbles first appear and when it starts to rise.
  8. Record the time it doubles in size.

Repeat this process once more with a different flour (like whole wheat) to see how it affects activity.

Recap

  • Feed your starter with equal parts flour and water by weight, not volume.
  • Store it at room temperature if baking daily, in the fridge if baking weekly.
  • A ready starter rises within 4–6 hours after feeding and smells sour, not rotten.

Check yourself

1. Why is it important to feed your sourdough starter with equal parts flour and water by weight rather than by volume?

Weight provides consistent and accurate ratios, while volume varies significantly with flour type and packing.

2. You plan to bake bread every 5 days. What is the best storage and feeding strategy for your starter?

Storing the starter in the fridge slows fermentation, making weekly feeding sufficient for infrequent bakers.

3. Your starter has a strong alcohol smell after being fed. What does this indicate, and what should you do?

A strong alcohol smell indicates over-fermentation, which means the starter has been left too long without feeding.

4. How can you tell if your sourdough starter is ready to use in bread dough?

A ready starter doubles in size within 4–6 hours after feeding, shows active bubbling, and has a pleasant sour or fruity smell.
